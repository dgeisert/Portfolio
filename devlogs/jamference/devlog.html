Behind the scenes of Jamference: how a free, fortnightly AI game jam reached 112 games and 876 player ratings in under two months, and why I record a playthrough of every single entry.

I started Jamference in October 2026. It's a game jam about making games with AI, out in the open: one week, one theme, one restriction, and then a week where everyone plays and rates everyone else's game. A new round starts every two weeks.

The short version for hiring managers is on my portfolio: Jamference case study. This is the long version.

Why I started it

I've been entering game jams for years. As AI tools got good, more and more of the jams I loved started banning games made with AI. I didn't want to stop jamming, so I set up my own: a jam where AI isn't just allowed, it's required, and everyone shows how they used it.

AI tools make it easy to get a game running in an afternoon. What they don't do is tell you whether it's fun. I kept seeing AI games that all reached for the same obvious idea, got posted, and were never played by anyone. I wanted a jam that would:

  • Push people past the obvious with a design restriction.
  • Get every game played by real players who would say what they thought.
  • Bring people back, so they get better from one round to the next.

The format

  • Theme plus restriction. The theme is optional inspiration. The restriction is mandatory, and you have to be able to explain how you met it in one sentence.
  • AI has to do real work. Code, art, audio, writing, level generation, or a live model in the game. "I used it for a variable name" doesn't count. Entrants list their tools and what each one did.
  • Players are the judges. Entrants rate each other on Fun, AI Use, Polish and Theme. Fun is listed first, because it's the whole point of making games.
  • Build it during the jam. No dusting off last month's prototype. Your own engine, tools and licensed or AI-generated assets are fine.

The restrictions

Writing the restriction is the part of the brief I spend the most time on. A good one is specific, easy to check, and changes the design on day one.

  • #1 Cat and Robot / Movement Input Only. Every input can only move the player: no attack button, no menus. Flappy Bird and Snake qualify; Vampire Survivors doesn't, because of the upgrade menu.
  • #2 Crafting / Playable on Mobile. Fully playable on a phone, straight from the itch page.
  • #3 Great Works of Art / No Text. Teach everything with art, sound and play.
  • #4 Solarpunk / Hidden Lucky Cat. Hide a maneki-neko somewhere a curious player can find it, and make finding it worth something.
  • #5 The Game is Out to Get You / Follow Open Game Rules. Revealed early, and lined up with the OpenGame.io jam so one game can enter both.

Each restriction gets a card that spells out exactly what counts.

The numbers

  • #1 Cat and Robot: 36 games, 349 ratings (9.7 per game)
  • #2 Crafting: 37 games, 305 ratings (8.2 per game)
  • #3 Great Works of Art: 27 games, 222 ratings (8.2 per game)
  • #4 Solarpunk: 12 games so far, with submissions open until Friday 20 November

The number I'm proudest of is ratings per game. In a lot of jams this size, plenty of entries get two or three ratings and some get none. In Jamference every round has averaged 8 to 10. People don't submit and leave; they stay and play each other's games.

Video feedback on every game

This is the part that takes the most time and matters the most. After every round I play every submitted game on video and talk through it as I go: what grabbed me in the first 30 seconds, where I didn't know what to do, where I got stuck, what I'd change. That's 100 videos for the first three rounds.

Star ratings tell you that something went wrong. Watching someone play tells you where. For a lot of entrants it's the first time they've watched a stranger play their game.

The best thing that's happened is that it has spread. The dedicated regulars have started leaving each other the same kind of detailed, specific feedback. Better feedback leads to better games, which brings more people who want to give back. It's a virtuous cycle, and it's what I'm proudest of.

The Discord

The Jamference Discord is where the jam actually lives between itch pages. It has 72 members, which isn't huge, but it's busy, during rounds and between them. A small, active server is worth far more to a jam than a big quiet one.

  • For the first four rounds the restriction dropped in the Discord the second the jam opened, so that's where everyone gathered at the starting bell.
  • Questions about the rules get answered there, and when a restriction turns out to be unclear I fix the wording for everyone.
  • Missed deadlines and broken builds get sorted out case by case. The earlier someone says something, the more I can do.
  • The tone is set by one rule: give constructive feedback, and don't bash anyone for how they use AI. AI game makers don't always get a warm welcome elsewhere, so this matters.

Regulars

The clearest sign it's working is who keeps coming back, and how well they do:

  • sykoramade won #1 with Neko Night Market and came 2nd in #2 with MULE.
  • Santo came 2nd in #1 with RoombaCat, then 2nd in #3 with Spears of Exekias, which was also rated the most fun game of that round.
  • QuantumWhisker won Use of AI in #1 with Sudo Meow and came 3rd in #2 with SNIP.
  • Atlas swept #2 with Mudlark Pottery: 1st overall, best Use of AI and best Polish.

What I changed along the way

  • Theme became a rated category in #3. In the first two rounds there were three categories: Fun, AI Use and Polish. Adding Theme rewards people who engage with it without making it compulsory.
  • Clearer rules mid-round. In #4 I clarified that entrants must say on their game page that a lucky cat is hidden, so raters know to go looking.
  • Restriction revealed early. For #5 the restriction is out before the jam opens. People get more build time, and the deadline doesn't move.
  • Keep updating during rating week. Feedback from the ratings and the videos can go straight back into the game.
  • First sponsor. For #5, Xorin is putting up $100 in prizes and $40 in credits for everyone.

How AI helps me run it

I run Jamference on my own, so I lean on AI for production. Gemini makes the animated pixel-art key art GIF for each round, like the ones at the top and bottom of this post. Claude, working as an agent, makes the promo video for each new round, the short videos announcing winners and spotlighting top games, and the restriction cards. That leaves my time for the parts that need a person: writing the brief, playing every game, and being in the Discord.

I enter too

I make a game every round and it's rated with everyone else's. Kitty Kingdom was rated the most fun game of #1, and I've kept building it into a roguelite. Starry Meows won #3. Being in the trenches tells me whether a brief is too hard, too loose or just right.

What's next

I want Jamference to be a community that helps each other grow as AI game designers, not just a place to submit a game. Two things come next:

  • Getting more people involved: sponsors, cross-entry with jams like OpenGame.io, and staying an easy first jam for newcomers.
  • Helping the regulars win elsewhere: using Jamference as a training ground for bigger jams with large payouts, and helping our builders place in them.
