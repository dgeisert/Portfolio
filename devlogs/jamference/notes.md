## 3-minute version (when it comes up)

**0:00 Hook (15 s).** "I founded and run Jamference, a free AI game jam every two weeks. Since October it's had 112 games and almost 900 player ratings, and I record a playthrough of every single entry."

**0:15 Origin (15 s).** "I'd been entering game jams for years, and they started banning AI games. So I set up my own, where AI is required."

**0:30 The design problem (30 s).** AI makes games fast but not fun. Three problems: everyone reaches for the obvious idea, nobody plays the result, and people make one game and leave. Each part of the format targets one.

**0:50 Format as design (50 s).** Theme is optional, restriction is mandatory: Movement Input Only, Playable on Mobile, No Text, Hidden Lucky Cat. A good restriction is specific, checkable and changes the design on day one. Rubric: Fun, AI Use, Polish, Theme, equally weighted, Fun first. Participants rate each other, so every game gets playtested by people who just solved the same brief.

**1:40 Community (50 s).** Video playthrough of every game: star ratings tell you *that* something's wrong, watching someone play tells you *where*. And it spread: regulars now give each other the same detailed feedback, a virtuous cycle. Discord is where the jam lives: 72 members and very active. Restriction dropped there at the starting bell, rules questions, late submissions handled case by case. One tone rule: constructive feedback, don't bash anyone for how they use AI. Evidence it works: about 9 ratings per game, and repeat builders keep placing (sykoramade 1st then 2nd; Santo 2nd, 2nd and Most Fun; QuantumWhisker AI Use then 3rd).

**2:30 Iteration (20 s).** Treat it like a product: added Theme as a category in #3, clarified rules mid-round, revealed the restriction early for #5, let people update during rating week, landed a first sponsor.

**2:45 What's next (10 s).** A community that helps each other grow as AI game designers, not just a place to submit: more people involved, and helping regulars win bigger jams with large payouts.

**2:55 Close (10 s).** "And I enter every round myself. Kitty Kingdom won Most Fun in #1 and Starry Meows won #3, so I know exactly how hard the brief is."

## Likely questions

### Ollie (design, process, playtesting)
- **How do you write a good restriction?** Specific, easy to check, and it should change the very first design decision. "No Text" works because everyone knows instantly whether they've met it. I write examples of what does and doesn't count (Flappy Bird yes, Vampire Survivors no, because of the upgrade menu).
- **What does a playthrough video give that ratings don't?** Location. Ratings are a score; a video shows the moment a player got confused, the first 30 seconds, where they quit. Same reason I'd run think-aloud sessions on a prototype.
- **How would you run this kind of thing inside a team?** The jam is a repeatable workshop format: a shared brief, a hard constraint, a time box, peer playtesting, and a show-and-tell. It maps directly onto a team brainstorm or prototyping sprint exploring new AI capabilities.
- **What did you change based on data?** Added Theme as a rated category, rewrote unclear restriction wording, moved the restriction reveal earlier to give more build time.
- **Why did #3 and #4 have fewer entries?** Be honest: cadence is fast (a new round every two weeks), and harder restrictions mean fewer but stronger entries. Ratings per game held up (8.2), which is the metric I care about. #5 changes the timing and adds a sponsor and the OpenGame.io tie-in to widen reach.

### Maggie (creative direction, art, AI art)
- **How do you set creative direction for 30+ strangers?** Through the brief: a theme paragraph with vivid examples ("a platformer painted in the swirling brushstrokes of The Starry Night"), plus "the theme is a launchpad, not a leash".
- **How does AI fit into running it?** Gemini makes the animated pixel-art key art GIF for each round (the same ginger cat and robot restaged for each theme, so the rounds read as a series). Claude makes the promo videos, winners and spotlight shorts and restriction cards. I spend my time on the brief, playing the games and the community.
- **What made the winning games stand out visually?** Mudlark Pottery swept #2 with best Polish and AI Use (4.615 Polish); Starry Meows won Polish and AI Use in #3. Strong art direction plus a clear constraint tends to win.

### Origin and community
- **Why start your own jam?** The jams I'd entered for years started banning AI games. Rather than argue or stop, I built the space I wanted, with rules that make AI use visible and judged on quality.
- **How do you build a feedback culture?** Model it. I gave detailed video feedback on every game; the regulars started doing the same for each other.
- **What's the long-term goal?** A community that helps each other grow as AI game designers. Next steps: grow participation, and help regulars win jams with large payouts.

### Harm / safety
- **AI-generated content risks?** Rules: no NSFW, harassment or hate; flag horror, gore or flashing imagery; credit assets and make sure you have the right to use them; disclose tools. Plus a culture rule: no bashing people for using AI.
