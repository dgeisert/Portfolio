How I built London Overgrown in five days for Jamference #4 (theme: Solarpunk, restriction: Hidden Lucky Cat). It's a pixel-art cycling RPG in the real Zone 1 of London, built from OpenStreetMap data. This post covers the brief, the day-by-day build, how I worked with Claude, the design decisions, and the 100 lucky cats.

The brief

Jamference #4 ran from 13 to 20 November 2026. The theme was Solarpunk and the restriction was Hidden Lucky Cat: hide a lucky cat somewhere in the game, and when the player finds it, give them a real benefit. I worked alone, as designer, creative director and playtester, with Claude writing the code.

I wanted a gritty but optimistic future, inspired by Becky Chambers' Monk and Robot books: a world that has been through something terrible and is quietly getting better. I also wanted the entire map to feel alive and worth exploring, every inch of London. I've enjoyed these museums and places so much in person, and I wanted that to really show in the game.

  • The tension: walls built out of fear. London is green and fed, but every faction lives behind a "hedge" of junk and sunflowers since the Magpie raids started.
  • The verb: delivery. Couriers are the only people who still cross. Every quest is someone who needs help from someone they don't trust, and you carry the first word over the wall.
  • The goal: Nan, the postmistress, wants every faction in one room for the Midsummer Moot. Each head says "yes, but…", and each "but" needs another faction's help, so the side quests are the main story.

Day by day

Day 1: the city

The first job was a pipeline that reads the OpenStreetMap extract for Greater London and writes 200 m tiles for Zone 1: building footprints with real heights and roof shapes, roads, canals, parks, trees, Tube entrances. Nearly 2,000 tiles in all, streamed in around the player and built in a web worker.

The first street out of the pipeline, on the morning of day 1.

Facades are generated with wave function collapse over a grid of bays and floors (shopfronts on the ground floor, windows lined up, party walls blank), then ivy, damage and wildflowers on top. That afternoon added narrowboats on the canals, wisteria on the terraces and fixes for bridges. By the evening the world bible was set and the story layer was going in.

Day 1: the canals got their narrowboats.

Day 2: something to do in it

Bikes, cargo strapped to your rack, deer and parakeet flocks, the Fate Reels dice system, the lucky cats, the first loners and the title-screen tour of the city. Then I rode around for a while and the map felt empty, which turned into the biggest decision of the jam (below).

Day 3: go inside anything

Every building got a generated interior, from terraced houses to Tube stations with abandoned trains, and the big museums got exhibits. Fishing went in. And a batch of checks made sure every gate, quest spot and Tube entrance could actually be reached on a bike.

Day 4: things to do between deliveries

The slingshot, the Brunswick Square Grand Prix and street sprints, catching animals in a crate for a stage magician, timed express post. Then the itch page and a 90-second trailer.

Day 5: feel and clarity

A UI redesign, quest markers over people's heads, a first-person camera, more detailed character faces and a tighter tutorial.

Working with Claude

Claude wrote the code: about 38,000 lines of JavaScript (three.js, no build step) and 3,700 lines of Python and Node tools. My job was direction. I set the premise, the factions and what each one wants, set the tone and the rules, played every build, and decided what stayed and what was cut. Claude drafted the dialogue against the world bible, and I reviewed it in play.

Art directed in words

There isn't a single texture or painted sprite in the game. Every model, from St Paul's to the pigeons, is built in code out of simple shapes, and a pixel shader gives it the look. So art direction meant description and reference. I worked from street photos of about 40 neighbourhoods, turned into style rules: white stucco with first-floor balconies in Belgravia, yellow-brick Georgian with black railings in Bloomsbury, red-brick mansion blocks in Kensington, warehouses with loading doors in Shad Thames. The music is generative too: a Web Audio score that gets busier as you ride faster and turns minor in a Magpie chase.

The one painted image is the logo, which I made with Gemini (Nano Banana). Claude then made the animated itch cover and header GIFs by layering the logo over gameplay footage.

Checkpoints

Before every risky change Claude saved a named checkpoint: "backup-before-bikes", "backup-before-first-person", "backup-before-railway". There were 62 by the end. It made bold experiments cheap, because rolling back took seconds.

Build the checkers too

When AI writes code this fast, the bottleneck is checking it. So Claude also built:

  • A story bot that validates every conversation link, item and quest stage, then plays the whole story hundreds of times with random choices and rolls to prove every ending is reachable and nothing soft-locks. It wins about 60% of its games.
  • Reachability audits for gates, Tube entrances, passages under buildings and quest spots.
  • Mini-game bots that check decent play wins and doing nothing loses.

No AI at runtime

Nothing in the game calls an AI model. The story is built on setups and payoffs planted hours apart (the clocks stopped at 3:07 only move in the best ending), so I wanted authored dialogue I could test. And building AI into a game only makes sense for something that will be supported live for a long time.

Four decisions

1. A real map, not a designed one

Real London gives you recognition and scale for free. But it isn't laid out for play. When the map felt empty, I had Claude grid it at 25 m and measure how far each spot was from anything to do. 70% of the land was more than 500 m from a landmark, character or faction, and the furthest point was 3.5 km out. That became a ranked list of real places to build in the biggest holes: Abbey Road, the Oval gasholders, Brompton Cemetery, Mount Pleasant sorting office, the Carreras factory and its giant cats. We built all of them and more, \1

2. Bring everyone together

Solarpunk is about an optimistic, eco-friendly future, and the best way to deliver on that was a story about everyone coming together to work towards a brighter one. So there are no enemies to defeat. Even the Magpie raiders are the children who were turned away from the gates in the Hungry Winter, and what their King wants is one word: sorry. The slingshot only stuns, and people hate it if you use it on them. Kind choices that are never the efficient ones open the best ending, where every faction, Magpies included, signs the Open Road Charter and Big Ben starts again. Raiders to fight would have been easier to make exciting, but they would have worked against the theme.

3. The restriction as a system

One hidden cat would have met the rule. I hid 100, at real London cat spots: Hodge's statue at Gough Square, cat cafés, the Carreras cats, under the great wisterias, at the far end of Tube platforms. Each gives +1 Luck, and every point of Luck adds 1% to every dice roll. Exploring the real city makes your courier better at talking their way through it.

4. Show the odds

Every check shows its odds and what failing costs before you commit, and locked replies show what they'd need. A failed roll rarely closes a door: it sends you the long way round.

What playtesting changed

I playtested every build, a few friends played it, and the Jamference Discord community played some of the earliest builds and told me what confused them.

  • The first five minutes. You should be riding, chased and delivering inside five. The tutorial is the most crucial place to iterate with the AI, as it can't approach the game with a lens of a new player.
  • Chase feel. Raider bikes are quicker than you cruise and slower than you sprint, so escape is always in your hands.
  • Empty lots read as holes, so they became overgrown rubble.
  • Fewer settings. Pixel size, colour grade and the day cycle are pinned to the look I wanted.

What I learned

Five days produced the biggest game I've ever made at a jam. With AI, the hard questions move from "can we build it?" to "does the player find the fun in five minutes?" and "does it hang together?" A clear world bible kept everything pointing the same way, and building the checkers caught problems I'd never have found by playing.

Next I'll watch the Jamference playthroughs and cut whatever players skip. I'd also like to try live AI in the townsfolk, answering free questions grounded in the city's history, with the guardrails I learned on Severed Heads, United Hearts.
